How does the FT232 chip turn USB into blinking serial bits?

Fig 4 · The chip in the plug

    The letter on the serial line right now. Light on = 0, so the line rests at 1. The little ticks on top are USB frames, 1 ms each: one letter at 300 baud lasts 33.3 of them.

    Machine colour

    The big idea: the chip is a translator with a bucket: USB pours letters in a millisecond at a time, and the chip lets them out one bit at a time at the speed you chose.

    Where the numbers come from
    • Buffers: the FT232R datasheet (v2.15, section 4.2) gives a 128-byte “FIFO RX buffer” for data from the USB OUT endpoint on its way to the UART transmitter, and a 256-byte “FIFO TX buffer” for data from the UART receiver to the USB IN endpoint. The names are seen from the USB side, so the 128 boxes feed the TX wire. The 2005 datasheet (v1.04) lists the two sizes the other way round.
    • Speed: “Baud Rate = 3000000 / (n + x)”, n an integer from 2 to 16384 and x a multiple of 0.125 (datasheet section 4.2; the 3 MHz is the 48 MHz clock divided by 16). So 300 baud is ÷10000 and 9600 baud is ÷312.5, from the same clock (computed here, matching the Linux driver's ftdi_232bm_baud_base_to_divisor).
    • USB: full speed, 12 Mbit/s (datasheet); a start-of-frame every 1 ms ± 500 ns; 8-bit SYNC, 8-bit PID, CRC16 and EOP in a data packet; bulk packets up to 64 bytes at full speed (USB in a NutShell, chapters 3 and 4). A 0 is stuffed after six 1s (USB 2.0, section 7.1.9). Counted here: the phone's packet is 77 bits with 2 stuffed (6.42 µs), the reply 59 bits (4.92 µs). The token and handshake packets around them are left out, and DATA0 out, DATA1 back is an assumption: the toggle depends on what came before.
    • Latency timer: 16 ms by default; when it runs out the chip sends its 2 status bytes and whatever data it holds; with 62 bytes waiting it sends at once (FTDI AN232B-04, and the comment in the Linux ftdi_sio.h). The timer counts from the last packet the chip sent, so the real wait after a letter lands is anywhere from 0 to 16 ms; the figure shows the longest. At 300 baud a letter takes 33.3 ms, longer than the timer, so each letter of the meter's reply travels to the phone in a parcel of its own.
    • Status bytes 01 60: byte 0 bit 0 is always 1; byte 1 bits 5 and 6 say the transmitter is empty (Linux ftdi_sio.h). 01 60 assumes the CTS, DSR, RI and DCD lines are all off, which is likely on this probe but not checked.
    • 300 baud 7E1 (10 bits a letter), light on = 0, the meter's 200 ms pause and its reply “/WPA5WM-ULTRA-15” come from figure 1 (IEC 62056-21 mode C; the meter is invented there). Only its first letter is shown arriving.
    • Simplified: the chip's bit counter runs all the time and the figure starts the first bit right after the packet; the real chip starts it within 1/16 of a bit, on its 16× clock. Letters moving between boxes and the trays are drawn slowly but take no real time. Pins: TXD is pin 1, RXD pin 5, USBDP and USBDM pins 15 and 16 on the 28-pin FT232RL (datasheet section 3); the rails, wheel and cabinets are a picture of what the chip does, not of its silicon.
